Diabetes mellitus

Why this disease is important

About one Nigerian adult in seventeen has diabetes mellitus, and the South-South has the most.

In one Nigerian teaching hospital, over half the adults admitted with a hyperglycaemic emergency had never been told they had diabetes.

About a third of patients admitted with a diabetic foot ulcer to six Nigerian teaching hospitals lost part of a limb.

Consider this case

A 56-year-old trader is brought to the emergency unit drowsy and breathing deeply. Some years ago she was told her sugar was high and given metformin, which she stopped when she felt well. Six weeks ago she burned her right foot while soaking it in hot water, and the wound has been treated at home with herbal dressings.

On examination she's dehydrated and her breath smells of acetone. There's a deep ulcer on the sole of her right foot, and the second toe is black and foul-smelling. She can't feel light touch or pinprick on either foot, and the foot pulses are weak.

Investigations: random plasma glucose 28 mmol/L; urine glucose 3+ and ketones 3+. Sodium 131, potassium 5.9, chloride 96 and bicarbonate 8 mmol/L; urea 14 mmol/L, creatinine 190 µmol/L. A malaria film is negative. A deep tissue sample from the ulcer base grows Staphylococcus aureus and Klebsiella pneumoniae, and an X-ray of the foot shows bone destruction under the ulcer. She's started on intravenous insulin and fluids. Once she's stable, her HbA1c is 11.2% and her urine albumin to creatinine ratio is raised. The toe is amputated, and her metformin isn't restarted while her creatinine stays high.

Definition of diabetes mellitus

In diabetes mellitus the blood glucose stays high and, over years, damages the vessels, kidneys, eyes and nerves.

Diabetes mellitus is a group of metabolic disorders with hyperglycaemia as the common feature, caused by too little insulin, , or both. In type 1 diabetes, autoimmune destruction of the β cells () leaves an absolute lack of insulin. In type 2 diabetes, insulin resistance meets β cells that can't keep up: a relative lack.

Quick checkType 1 and type 2 diabetes mellitus start in different ways. Why do both end with the same damage to the kidney, eye, nerve and arteries?

Hyperglycaemia. Whatever its cause, glucose that stays high drives of proteins and thickens small vessels (), so the long-term complications are the same in both types.

Histology of diabetes mellitus

Key facts

  • Nodular glomerulosclerosis (Kimmelstiel-Wilson nodules) is almost pathognomonic of diabetes mellitus.
  • Hyaline arteriolosclerosis of the efferent arteriole is almost unique to diabetes mellitus.
  • In diabetes mellitus capillary basement membranes thicken throughout the body, yet the capillaries leak.

Down the microscope, diabetes mellitus thickens basement membranes, scars glomeruli and narrows arterioles. The islets change too, differently in each type.

Differential diagnosis of diabetes mellitus

Key facts

  • Glomerular amyloid shows apple-green birefringence with Congo red; a Kimmelstiel-Wilson nodule doesn't.
  • Severe illness raises the glucose for a while, so diabetes needs hyperglycaemia that persists after recovery.
  • Chronic pancreatitis, Cushing syndrome, acromegaly and glucocorticoids all cause secondary diabetes.

Diabetes mellitus has differentials at two levels: other nodules in the glomerulus, and other causes of a high blood glucose.

Kimmelstiel-Wilson nodules and renal amyloid both put pink material in the glomerulus, and both can cause the nephrotic syndrome.

Aetiology of diabetes mellitus

Key facts

  • Type 1 diabetes is linked to HLA-DR3 and HLA-DR4, but most people with these genes never develop it.
  • Type 2 diabetes comes from genes plus obesity, central obesity above all, and physical inactivity.
  • Type 2 diabetes has no HLA link, yet identical twins share it more often than they share type 1.

Pathogenesis of diabetes mellitus

Key facts

  • In type 1 diabetes T cells destroy the β cells, and symptoms appear only once most are gone.
  • In type 2 diabetes the β cells first compensate for insulin resistance, then fail.
  • Advanced glycation end products, protein kinase C and the polyol pathway link high glucose to the complications.

Type 1 and type 2 diabetes mellitus reach hyperglycaemia by different routes. Hyperglycaemia then damages vessels and nerves in the same way in both.

Pathogenesis of type 1 diabetes, step by step

Step 1 of 5

Genetic susceptibility

Class II HLA genes, HLA-DR3 and HLA-DR4, carry the strongest risk, with smaller effects from CTLA4, PTPN22 and the insulin gene. Most people who inherit them never develop diabetes, so the genes predispose without causing it.

Self-tolerance fails

T cells that react against β cell antigens escape deletion in the thymus, and regulatory T cells fail to hold them back. An infection, possibly a virus, may start the attack, but its role is unclear.

T cells destroy β cells

Effector T cells infiltrate the islets and kill the β cells: a type IV hypersensitivity reaction. Early in the disease the islets show β cell necrosis and lymphocytes, called .

Autoantibodies appear

Antibodies against insulin and glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD) are found in most patients, often before symptoms begin. They mark the attack, but it isn't clear that they cause the damage.

Most β cells gone

Symptoms appear late, once most β cells are destroyed, and often abruptly, when an infection raises the need for insulin. Without insulin, fat breaks down unchecked and follows.

Pathogenesis of type 2 diabetes, step by step

Step 1 of 4

Obesity

Obesity is the strongest driver of insulin resistance, and central, abdominal fat more than fat under the skin, because it releases more free fatty acids. Genes that affect insulin secretion add to the risk.

Fatty acids, adipokines, inflammation

Fatty acids build up inside muscle and liver cells and block insulin signalling. Fat also releases adipokines, and less adiponectin, which normally improves insulin sensitivity. Excess nutrients set off inflammation, and cytokines such as interleukin-1 add to the resistance.

Insulin resistance

in three tissues. The liver keeps making glucose, so the fasting glucose rises. Muscle takes up less glucose after a meal. Fat keeps breaking down and releases more fatty acids, which make the resistance worse.

β cells compensate, then fail

At first the β cells secrete more insulin and keep the glucose normal. Over years they fail: high glucose (glucotoxicity), excess fatty acids (lipotoxicity), a weak incretin effect and amyloid in the islets all reduce secretion. Relative insulin deficiency and hyperglycaemia follow.

Pathogenesis of the chronic complications of diabetes mellitus

  1. Nerves, lens, kidney and blood vessels take up glucose without needing insulin. When the blood glucose stays high, glucose inside their cells rises too, and three pathways turn it into damage.

  2. Glucose-derived molecules bind proteins without an enzyme, forming . Through their receptor they release TGF-β, which lays down basement membrane, and VEGF. They also cross-link matrix, trapping LDL in artery walls and albumin in capillary walls.

  3. High glucose inside cells raises diacylglycerol, which activates protein kinase C. Protein kinase C drives VEGF, which makes the new vessels of diabetic retinopathy, and TGF-β, which thickens basement membranes.

  4. Aldose reductase turns excess glucose into sorbitol, using up NADPH. Without NADPH, glutathione can't be regenerated, and the cell is left open to oxidative stress: the .

  5. In small vessels the result is basement membranes that are thick but leaky (). In large arteries it's accelerated atherosclerosis. These two lesions underlie the kidney, eye, nerve and foot complications.

Signs and symptoms of diabetes mellitus

Key facts

  • Polyuria, polydipsia and polyphagia are the classic triad; polyphagia with weight loss suggests diabetes.
  • Above the renal threshold, glucose spills into the urine and holds water with it: osmotic diuresis.
  • Type 1 diabetes starts abruptly in a lean patient; type 2 diabetes is insidious and often found late.

Classic symptoms of diabetes mellitus, step by step

Step 1 of 5

Glucose stays high

Without insulin action, muscle and fat take up little glucose, glycogen stores are broken down, and the liver keeps making glucose. The plasma glucose rises.

Glycosuria

Once the plasma glucose passes the renal threshold, the tubules can't reabsorb it all, and glucose appears in the urine.

Polyuria

Glucose in the tubule holds water with it: an . The patient passes large volumes of urine and loses water and electrolytes.

Polydipsia

Water loss and the high plasma osmolality dehydrate cells, including the osmoreceptors in the brain, and the patient becomes intensely thirsty.

Polyphagia with weight loss

Without insulin, protein and fat are broken down for fuel. The negative energy balance drives hunger, but breakdown wins: the patient eats more and still loses weight and muscle.

Course of diabetes mellitus

Diabetes mellitus does most of its harm slowly: the chronic complications appear years after hyperglycaemia begins.

Remember. When the chronic complications of diabetes mellitus start, how severe they are and which organs they hit vary widely between patients.

Quick checkWhat do people with diabetes mellitus most often die of?

Myocardial infarction, from atherosclerosis of the coronary arteries. Renal failure comes second. In diabetes, myocardial infarction is almost as common in women as in men, though it's rare in women of reproductive age without diabetes.

Complications of diabetes mellitus

Key facts

  • Macrovascular disease in diabetes is accelerated atherosclerosis: myocardial infarction, stroke and gangrene.
  • Microvascular disease in diabetes damages the kidney, retina and nerves: nephropathy, retinopathy, neuropathy.
  • Proliferative diabetic retinopathy follows when ischaemic retina makes VEGF and new vessels sprout.

The chronic complications of diabetes mellitus are grouped by the size of the vessel damaged: large arteries or small vessels.

Diabetic foot ulcer and gangrene

Key facts

  • Diabetic foot ulcers are neuropathic, ischaemic or both; infection then drives them towards gangrene.
  • With diabetic neuropathy, a trivial toe infection can lead on to gangrene, bacteraemia and death.
  • In Nigerian patients, an ulcer over a month old, peripheral arterial disease and osteomyelitis predicted amputation.

A diabetic foot ulcer is neuropathic, ischaemic or both, and infection often complicates it once it's open.

Susceptibility to infection in diabetes mellitus

Key facts

  • Sensory neuropathy numbs the feet in diabetes mellitus, so a burn or cut can go unfelt.
  • Diabetes mellitus delays wound healing, so a small wound stays open for longer.
  • A neurogenic bladder leaves urine standing, so urinary infection is commoner in diabetes mellitus.

Diabetes mellitus makes infection more likely and wounds slower to heal, and damaged nerves let a wound start without the patient noticing.

Infections in diabetes mellitus

Key facts

  • An infected diabetic foot ulcer often grows several organisms, and in a Nigerian series most of the isolates were Gram-negative rods.
  • Rhinocerebral mucormycosis, caused by Rhizopus and Mucor, invades blood vessels and is typical of diabetic ketoacidosis.
  • Osteomyelitis under a diabetic foot ulcer is often mixed, because it spreads from the ulcer.

Diabetes mellitus raises the risk of skin infection, tuberculosis, pneumonia and pyelonephritis. A few infections are typical of it, and each has its own organisms.

Glucose homeostasis

Key facts

  • Insulin is the main hormone that lowers glucose; glucagon, adrenaline, cortisol and growth hormone raise it.
  • Proinsulin splits into insulin and C-peptide in equal amounts, so C-peptide marks the body's own insulin.
  • In a fast, low insulin lets fat break down, and the liver turns the fatty acids into ketones.

In glucose homeostasis, insulin and the hormones that oppose it keep plasma glucose in a narrow range, after a meal and through a fast.

Key facts

  • Diabetes is diagnosed by a fasting venous plasma glucose of 7.0 mmol/L or more, or a random one of 11.1 or more.
  • With symptoms one high glucose is enough; without symptoms the test is repeated on another day.
  • Glycosuria never diagnoses diabetes mellitus, because a low renal threshold or pregnancy can cause it too.

Diabetes mellitus is diagnosed on venous plasma glucose. A fasting or a random sample is usually enough, and symptoms decide whether one result will do.

Oral glucose tolerance test

Key facts

  • An OGTT measures venous plasma glucose fasting and 2 hours after 75 g of anhydrous glucose.
  • An OGTT is read on the diagnostic thresholds: fasting 7.0 or 2-hour 11.1 mmol/L or more means diabetes.
  • Carbohydrate restriction, an afternoon test, steroids, thiazides and the oral contraceptive can upset an OGTT.

The oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) settles fasting or random results that fall between normal and diabetic, including in a pregnant woman at high risk of gestational diabetes.

Quick checkWhen is an OGTT needed instead of a single plasma glucose?

Oral glucose tolerance test, step by step

  1. The patient eats a normal diet for the few days before the test. A low-carbohydrate diet, such as one for weight loss, shifts metabolism towards fasting and makes glucose tolerance look worse than it is.

  2. The patient fasts overnight, drinking only water, and the test starts in the morning. Reference values are for morning tests; afternoon tests give higher glucose results.

  3. A venous sample is taken for fasting plasma glucose, into a fluoride oxalate tube so the cells can't use up glucose before the plasma is separated.

  4. The patient drinks the equivalent of 75 g of anhydrous glucose in water, slowly, over a few minutes. The patient rests and doesn't smoke for the rest of the test.

  5. A second venous sample is taken 2 hours after the drink. Fasting and 2-hour results are read against the same thresholds used for diagnosis.

Key facts

  • In DKA, lipolysis floods the liver with fatty acids, and the ketones it makes use up bicarbonate.

Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) follows a severe lack of insulin. Fat breaks down, ketones build up, and every result traces back to a step in that chain.

Pathogenesis of diabetic ketoacidosis, step by step

Step 1 of 5

Insulin runs out

Missed insulin, an infection or a first presentation leaves too little insulin. Stress hormones such as adrenaline make it worse, because they oppose what insulin is left and release glucagon.

Glucose climbs

Glucose can't enter muscle and fat, and the liver keeps making more. Above the renal threshold it spills into the urine.

Water and salts are lost

Glucose in the urine pulls water with it: an osmotic diuresis. Water, sodium and potassium are lost, vomiting adds to the loss, and the GFR falls. Water leaves brain cells too, which causes confusion and coma.

Fat breaks down

Without insulin, lipolysis runs unchecked. More fatty acids reach the liver than the tissues can burn, and the liver turns them into ketones: acetoacetate, β-hydroxybutyrate and acetone.

Bicarbonate runs out

Bicarbonate buffers the hydrogen ions made with the ketones until production outruns it, and bicarbonate falls. Ketone anions take its place, so the widens. and acetone on the breath follow.

He probably still makes some insulin: enough to stop lipolysis, so no ketones form, but not enough to stop gluconeogenesis or move glucose into cells, so glucose climbs.

Anion gap and lactic acidosis

Key facts

  • Anion gap = (Na⁺ + K⁺) − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻), normally about 15–20 mmol/L.
  • A high anion gap means an added acid: ketones, lactate, uraemia, salicylate, methanol or ethylene glycol.
  • Lactic acidosis is type A from poor tissue perfusion, or type B from organ failure, drugs or inborn errors.

The anion gap estimates the anions the laboratory doesn't measure. In a metabolic acidosis a high gap means an acid has been added, and it helps name the acid.

Key facts

  • Microalbuminuria, a urine albumin of 30–300 mg a day, is one of the earliest signs of diabetic nephropathy.
  • Microalbuminuria is measured as the albumin to creatinine ratio on a spot urine and confirmed on repeat samples.
  • Microalbuminuria predicts both progressive kidney disease and cardiovascular disease.

In diabetic nephropathy albumin appears in the urine early, while kidney function still looks normal, and the loss grows as the kidney fails.

Classes of glucose-lowering drugs

Key facts

  • Insulin, sulfonylureas and incretin drugs raise insulin; metformin and pioglitazone help insulin work.
  • SGLT2 inhibitors pass glucose into the urine, and acarbose slows its absorption from the gut.
  • Insulin and sulfonylureas cause hypoglycaemia; metformin and pioglitazone don't on their own.

Glucose-lowering drugs are grouped by how they lower glucose, and a drug's group predicts most of what it does.

The main glucose-lowering drugs work in one of four ways: they add insulin, release more insulin from the β cells, make the liver and tissues respond better to insulin, or remove glucose by another route.

Each group lowers glucose in a different way, and the way predicts whether it causes hypoglycaemia.

Insulin: mechanism and adverse effects

Key facts

  • Insulin binds a tyrosine kinase receptor and moves GLUT4 to the membrane of muscle and fat cells.
  • Hypoglycaemia is the commonest complication of insulin treatment.
  • Insulin drives potassium into cells, so plasma potassium falls when insulin is given in ketoacidosis.

Insulin replaces the hormone the β cells no longer make, so every patient with type 1 diabetes mellitus needs it. Insulin's adverse effects follow from what insulin does and from how it's injected.

Connect this to other specialties
Diabetic ketoacidosis

Mechanism of insulin action

  1. The insulin receptor sits on liver, muscle and fat cells. Insulin binds its α subunits, outside the cell, and the receptor changes shape.

  2. Each β subunit, which crosses the membrane, carries a tyrosine kinase. Once insulin binds, the β subunits phosphorylate each other and switch on a chain of kinases inside the cell.

  3. The signal moves transporters from inside the cell to the membrane, so muscle and fat take up glucose. Glycogen synthesis rises.

  4. Insulin inhibits glycogen breakdown and gluconeogenesis in the liver, and lipolysis in fat. With less lipolysis, the liver makes fewer ketone bodies.

Types of insulin

Metformin: mechanism and adverse effects

Key facts

  • Metformin lowers glucose mainly by reducing gluconeogenesis in the liver.
  • Metformin is excreted unchanged by the kidney, so in renal failure it builds up and raises the risk of lactic acidosis.
  • Metformin doesn't cause hypoglycaemia on its own; gut upset is its commonest adverse effect.

Metformin is started at diagnosis of type 2 diabetes mellitus in Nigeria unless something rules it out. It leaves the body unchanged through the kidney, so kidney failure lets it build up.

Mechanism of metformin-associated lactic acidosis

  1. Metformin's main effect is to reduce in the liver. The full mechanism isn't known.

  2. The liver uses lactate to make new glucose. With gluconeogenesis reduced, the liver may clear less lactate.

  3. Metformin isn't bound to plasma proteins or metabolised. The kidney excretes it unchanged, so in renal failure it builds up.

  4. Hypoxic tissue makes more lactic acid, so heart or lung failure adds to the load. Liver failure and alcohol excess also raise the risk.

  5. Lactate accumulates and causes a high anion gap metabolic acidosis: . It's rare, and almost all cases had a risk factor that should have stopped the drug.

Sulfonylureas: mechanism and hypoglycaemia

Key facts

  • Sulfonylureas close the β cell's ATP-sensitive potassium channel, which releases stored insulin.
  • Hypoglycaemia and weight gain are the commonest adverse effects of sulfonylureas.
  • Glibenclamide acts for over a day and isn't used in chronic kidney disease or liver failure.

Sulfonylureas make the β cell release the insulin it has stored, so they work only while β cells are left. Their main harm is hypoglycaemia.

Connect this to other specialties
Hypoglycaemia

Mechanism of sulfonylureas: insulin release from the β cell

  1. After a meal, glucose enters the β cell and is metabolised, and ATP inside the cell rises.

  2. Rising ATP closes the . A sulfonylurea closes the same channel directly, by binding the sulfonylurea receptor on it, whatever the glucose.

  3. Potassium can no longer leave the β cell, so its membrane depolarises.

  4. Depolarisation opens voltage-gated calcium channels, and calcium flows into the β cell.

  5. Calcium triggers release of the insulin already made and stored in the β cell. With no β cells left, as in type 1 diabetes, there's nothing to release.

Tell me the differences between type 1 and type 2 diabetes mellitus.

Type 1 diabetes is autoimmune destruction of the β cells, linked to HLA-DR3 and DR4, with islet autoantibodies, insulitis and an absolute lack of insulin; it usually starts young in a lean patient. Type 2 diabetes is insulin resistance with failing β cells, linked to obesity and to genes outside HLA, with islet amyloid late in the disease.

  1. Which acute complication goes with each type?

    Usually diabetic ketoacidosis with type 1 diabetes and the hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state with type 2.

  2. Why does obesity cause insulin resistance?

    Fat, central fat above all, releases free fatty acids that block insulin signalling in muscle and liver. It also releases adipokines, makes less adiponectin, and sets off inflammation.

Tell me how high glucose damages blood vessels in diabetes mellitus.

Through three pathways. Advanced glycation end products cross-link matrix and, through their receptor, release TGF-β and VEGF. High glucose inside cells activates protein kinase C, which raises VEGF and TGF-β too. The polyol pathway uses up NADPH, so glutathione falls and oxidative stress rises.

  1. Why are the nerves, lens and kidney hit when the glucose is high?

    They take up glucose without needing insulin, so glucose inside their cells rises with the blood glucose.

  2. How does glycation speed up atherosclerosis?

    Advanced glycation end products cross-link proteins in the walls of large arteries, and the cross-linked proteins trap LDL.

    Dry gangrene is coagulative necrosis of a limb that has lost its blood supply: black, shrunken and dry. In wet gangrene bacterial infection is added, and the tissue liquefies.

  2. How can a trivial toe infection kill a patient with diabetes?

    In a foot with neuropathy, the infection goes unnoticed and can lead on to gangrene, bacteraemia and pneumonia, and then death.

    Sensory neuropathy hides the wound, and diabetes mellitus delays its healing, so it stays open.

  1. How do you tell it from Aspergillus on a slide?

    Aspergillus hyphae have septa and branch at acute angles. Mucormycosis hyphae have no septa and branch at right angles.

  2. Where does the rhinocerebral form spread?

    The moulds settle in the nasal cavity or sinuses, then spread directly into the orbit, the cranial bones and the brain.

Tell me why urinary infection is commoner in diabetes mellitus.

Autonomic neuropathy causes a neurogenic bladder that doesn't empty, so urine stands and bacteria multiply. Bladder dysfunction also allows reflux, which carries infected urine up to the kidney, and patients with diabetes are more susceptible to infection in general. Chemical Pathology

Tell me what you know about diabetic ketoacidosis.

Diabetic ketoacidosis follows a severe lack of insulin. Lipolysis sends fatty acids to the liver, which turns them into ketones, and their hydrogen ions use up bicarbonate, giving a high anion gap metabolic acidosis. The hyperglycaemia causes an osmotic diuresis and dehydration.

  1. Why is the plasma potassium often high when the total body potassium is low?

    Without insulin, and with the acidosis, potassium moves out of cells, and a low GFR keeps it in the blood. Meanwhile the osmotic diuresis has been losing potassium in the urine, so the body's store is low.

  2. Why is the sodium low?

    The high glucose draws water out of the cells, which dilutes the sodium. A normal or high sodium beside a very high glucose means heavy water loss.

  3. Why can a urine ketone strip underestimate the ketones in DKA?

    Nitroprusside strips detect acetoacetate best, and β-hydroxybutyrate doesn't always react, so part of the ketone load can be missed.

How is diabetes mellitus diagnosed in the laboratory?

On venous plasma glucose. A fasting glucose of 7.0 mmol/L or more, or a random glucose of 11.1 or more, is diabetic. With symptoms one result is enough; without symptoms the test is repeated on another day. An OGTT settles equivocal results.

  1. Can you diagnose diabetes from glucose in the urine?

    No. Glycosuria never makes the diagnosis, because it also comes with a low renal threshold, pregnancy and glucose infusions.

  2. What do you call a fasting glucose between 6.1 and 6.9 mmol/L?

    Impaired fasting glucose, as long as the 2-hour OGTT result is below 7.8. It's a stage between normal and diabetes mellitus.

  3. Why does the sample go into a fluoride oxalate tube?

    Blood cells keep using glucose in the tube. Fluoride stops glycolysis, so a delayed sample doesn't read falsely low.

How would you investigate hypoglycaemia in an adult without diabetes?

First confirm it: a plasma glucose below 2.5 mmol/L in a fluoride tube, with Whipple's triad. Then measure insulin and C-peptide while the glucose is low. High insulin with low C-peptide means injected insulin; both high means an insulinoma or a sulfonylurea, so screen for the drug.

  1. Why does C-peptide tell injected insulin from the body's own?

    The β cell splits proinsulin into insulin and C-peptide in equal amounts. Injected insulin carries no C-peptide, so C-peptide is low.

  2. A plain-tube sample reads 1.9 mmol/L in a well patient. What happened?

    Pseudohypoglycaemia. The blood cells kept using glucose in the tube before the plasma was separated.

How do you tell diabetic ketoacidosis from the hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state?

HHS affects mainly older people with type 2 diabetes. The glucose is usually far higher, the sodium and osmolality are high from water loss, and there are no significant ketones, so the bicarbonate is near normal. In DKA ketones are present and the bicarbonate is low.

  1. Why are there no ketones in HHS?

    Probably because there's enough insulin to stop lipolysis, so no ketones form, but not enough to stop gluconeogenesis, so the glucose climbs.

  2. How do you calculate plasma osmolality?

    Twice the sodium, plus twice the potassium, plus urea, plus glucose, all in mmol/L. Some versions leave out potassium.

Pharmacology

Tell me what you know about metformin.

Metformin is a biguanide. It lowers glucose mainly by reducing gluconeogenesis in the liver. It doesn't cause hypoglycaemia on its own. Gastrointestinal upset is its commonest adverse effect, and lactic acidosis is rare but serious.

  1. Why is metformin not given when the kidneys fail?

    The kidney excretes metformin unchanged. In renal failure it accumulates, and because it reduces gluconeogenesis the liver may clear less lactate, so lactic acidosis becomes likelier.

  2. What happens with years of metformin use?

    Metformin interferes with vitamin B12 absorption in the terminal ileum, so B12 deficiency can develop, with neuropathy or a macrocytic anaemia.

Tell me how sulfonylureas work.

Sulfonylureas bind the sulfonylurea receptor on the β cell's ATP-sensitive potassium channel and close it. The membrane depolarises, calcium channels open and calcium enters, and the β cell releases its stored insulin. They need working β cells.

  1. What are their main adverse effects?

    Hypoglycaemia and weight gain are the commonest adverse effects of sulfonylureas.

  2. Who is most at risk of hypoglycaemia on glibenclamide?

    The elderly, and patients with chronic kidney disease or liver failure. Glibenclamide's effect lasts over a day because its metabolites are active and the drug is held in the β cell.

Tell me what you know about the adverse effects of insulin.

Hypoglycaemia is the commonest complication of insulin. Frequent episodes can cause hypoglycaemia unawareness. Injecting into the same site causes lipohypertrophy. Insulin drives potassium into cells, so it can cause hypokalaemia.

  1. How does insulin get glucose into muscle and fat?

    Insulin binds its receptor, a tyrosine kinase. The signal moves GLUT4 transporters to the membrane of muscle and fat cells, so glucose enters.

  2. Why does plasma potassium fall when insulin is given in ketoacidosis?

    Insulin drives potassium into cells, and total body potassium is already low from the osmotic diuresis, so plasma potassium can fall sharply.

Tell me what you know about SGLT2 inhibitors.

SGLT2 inhibitors, such as dapagliflozin, block glucose reabsorption in the proximal tubule, so glucose is lost in the urine. They cause genital fungal and urinary infections, volume depletion, and euglycaemic ketoacidosis.

  1. Why is ketoacidosis on an SGLT2 inhibitor easy to miss?

    The urine keeps removing glucose, so the plasma glucose can be normal or only a little raised while ketones build up and bicarbonate falls.

    A plasma protein, such as CRP or fibrinogen, that the liver makes in larger amounts when cytokines signal inflammation.

    Products formed when glucose-derived molecules bind proteins without an enzyme. In diabetes mellitus they cross-link matrix proteins and act on their receptor, RAGE, to damage vessel walls.

    The formation of antibodies against antigens on transfused red cells that the patient's own red cells lack.

    A mild, normocytic anaemia of long-lasting infection, inflammation or cancer. Hepcidin holds iron in macrophages, so the marrow is short of iron while stores are full.

    Sodium plus potassium, minus chloride and bicarbonate, in the plasma. It stands for the anions that aren't measured, and it widens when an acid such as lactate replaces bicarbonate.
    Anitschkow cell (caterpillar cell)
    A plump, activated macrophage in an Aschoff body, with abundant cytoplasm and chromatin condensed into a slender, wavy ribbon down the middle of the nucleus.
    Apoptosis
    Regulated cell death: the cell switches on enzymes that dismantle it from within, in pieces that are eaten without inflammation.
    Aschoff body
    A focus of inflammation pathognomonic of acute rheumatic fever: T lymphocytes, scattered plasma cells and Anitschkow cells around a zone of fibrinoid necrosis.
    ASO titre (anti-streptolysin O titre)
    The level of antibody against streptolysin O in serum. A raised titre shows a recent group A streptococcal infection.
    Atherosclerosis
    Disease of large and medium-sized arteries in which raised plaques, a lipid core under a fibrous cap, form in the intima and narrow the lumen.
    Atrophy
    A decrease in the size of an organ or tissue, from smaller cells, fewer cells, or both.
    Autosplenectomy
    Loss of the spleen to repeated infarcts, which shrink it to a small nubbin of fibrous tissue. In sickle cell disease it's complete by adulthood.
    Avascular necrosis (osteonecrosis)
    Infarction of bone and marrow. Under a joint, a wedge of dead bone forms and can collapse, as in the femoral head.
    Beta-haemolysis (β-haemolysis)
    Complete lysis of the red cells in blood agar around a bacterial colony, leaving a clear zone. Alpha-haemolysis is partial lysis, with a green zone.
    Blackwater fever
    Sudden intravascular haemolysis in falciparum malaria, with haemoglobin in the urine turning it dark, and acute kidney injury.
    Blood schizonticide
    An antimalarial drug that kills the asexual parasites inside red cells, the stage that causes the illness. Every drug that treats malaria is one.
    C-peptide
    A peptide the pancreas releases along with insulin. It's raised when the patient's own pancreas makes too much insulin, and suppressed when insulin is injected.
    Caseous necrosis (caseation)
    Necrosis in which dead tissue loses all structure and becomes soft, granular and cheese-like. Down the microscope it has no nuclei and no preserved architecture.
    Caspase
    One of a family of enzymes that carry out apoptosis.
    CD21
    A receptor on B cells for the complement fragment C3d. Epstein-Barr virus binds CD21 to get into the B cell.
    CD40
    A receptor on B cells. A helper T cell engages it through CD40 ligand to make the B cell divide. LMP1 of Epstein-Barr virus mimics CD40 and is switched on all the time.
    Cell cycle–nonspecific drug
    A cytotoxic drug that kills cells whether they're cycling or resting in G0, such as cyclophosphamide or doxorubicin. Cycling cells are still more sensitive to it.
    Cell cycle–specific drug
    A cytotoxic drug that kills cells only while they pass through one phase of the cell cycle: methotrexate and cytarabine in S phase, vincristine in M phase.
    Cell-mediated immunity
    Defence carried out by T cells. Sensitised T cells activate macrophages so that they can kill the organisms they have phagocytosed.
    Cinchonism
    Tinnitus, headache, nausea, dizziness, flushing and blurred vision caused by quinine at treatment doses. Quinine comes from the bark of the cinchona tree.
    Class switching
    A change in the heavy-chain constant region a B cell uses, for example from IgM to IgG. It cuts and rejoins the heavy-chain gene in the germinal centre.
    Coagulative necrosis
    Necrosis in which the dead cells keep their outline for some days, so the tissue's architecture can still be seen.
    Cofactor
    A cause that helps a tumour arise but can't produce it alone. Epstein-Barr virus is a cofactor in endemic Burkitt lymphoma.
    Commissural fusion
    Scarring that joins neighbouring valve leaflets or cusps at their edges, so the valve can't open fully. It's a sign of past inflammation.
    Counter-regulatory hormones
    Hormones that raise plasma glucose and oppose insulin: glucagon, adrenaline, cortisol and growth hormone.
    Culture-negative endocarditis
    Infective endocarditis in which no organism grows from blood cultures, usually because antibiotics were given first or the organism is hard to grow.
    Cyclooxygenase (COX)
    The enzyme that turns arachidonic acid into prostaglandins and, in platelets, thromboxane. Aspirin and other NSAIDs block it, which lowers inflammation, pain and fever.
    Cytochrome P450
    A family of liver enzymes that break down many drugs, warfarin among them. A drug that inhibits these enzymes raises the level of other drugs; a drug that induces them lowers it.
    Dactylitis (hand-foot syndrome)
    Painful swelling of the hands and feet caused by infarcts of their small bones. It's often the first presentation of sickle cell disease.
    Dihydrofolate reductase
    The enzyme that reduces dihydrofolate back to tetrahydrofolate, the active form of folate used to make DNA. Proguanil blocks the malaria parasite's own form of it.
    Duffy antigen (DARC)
    A chemokine receptor on the red cell surface. Plasmodium vivax uses it to enter the red cell, and most people of West African origin lack it.
    Dystrophic calcification
    Calcium deposited in dead or damaged tissue while the blood calcium is normal.
    EBER (EBV-encoded small RNA)
    Small RNA molecules made in large amounts by Epstein-Barr virus in a latently infected cell. A stain for EBER on a tissue section shows which cells carry the virus.
    Encapsulated bacteria
    Bacteria wrapped in a polysaccharide capsule that resists phagocytosis, such as Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae type b and Neisseria meningitidis. Antibody against the capsule lets phagocytes take them up.
    Endoperoxide bridge
    The bridge of two linked oxygen atoms in the artemisinin molecule. Iron is thought to split it, and the free radicals released kill the malaria parasite.
    Epithelioid macrophage
    An activated macrophage with abundant cytoplasm and an elongated nucleus. These cells form the wall of the granuloma.
    Erythema marginatum
    A red, ring-shaped rash on the skin, one of the major Jones criteria for acute rheumatic fever.
    Euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis
    Diabetic ketoacidosis with a plasma glucose that's normal or only a little raised. SGLT2 inhibitors can cause it in patients who are very short of insulin.
    Extranodal
    Arising outside the lymph nodes, in an organ such as the jaw, bowel or kidney. Burkitt lymphoma usually arises at extranodal sites.
    Extravascular haemolysis
    Destruction of red cells by macrophages, rather than inside the blood vessels. It causes jaundice and pigment gallstones.
    Fat necrosis
    Death of fat cells, from released lipases or from injury, with chalky white deposits where fatty acids bind calcium.
    Fc receptor
    A receptor on macrophages, neutrophils and other leucocytes that binds the tail (Fc part) of an antibody, so the cell can engulf or attack what the antibody has coated.
    Ferritin
    The protein that stores iron. Its plasma level usually follows the iron stores, but it's an acute-phase protein, so inflammation raises it too.
    Fetal warfarin syndrome
    Birth defects, chiefly abnormal bone formation, after a fetus is exposed to warfarin early in pregnancy. Warfarin crosses the placenta and blocks the carboxylation of fetal bone proteins.
    Fibrinoid necrosis
    Necrosis of a vessel wall where immune complexes or leaked plasma proteins are deposited, staining bright pink.
    Fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH)
    A test that binds fluorescent DNA probes to chosen genes in a cell's nucleus. A probe pair can show a gene broken apart or moved to a new chromosome, even in cells that aren't dividing.
    Flow cytometry
    A test that reads fluorescent antibodies bound to cells in suspension, cell by cell. It works on blood, marrow aspirate or disaggregated lymph node tissue.
    Free radical
    A molecule with an unpaired electron, which reacts with and damages lipids, proteins and DNA.
    Fructosamine
    Glucose bound to plasma proteins, mostly albumin. It reflects glucose control over the last few weeks, and it's used when HbA1c can't be trusted.
    Functional asplenia (hyposplenism)
    A spleen that's present but can't do its work of filtering the blood and clearing bacteria. In sickle cell disease it starts in early childhood, while the spleen is still large.
    Gametocide
    An antimalarial drug that kills the sexual forms of the parasite, the gametocytes, so they can't pass the infection on to a mosquito.
    Gametocyte
    The male or female sexual form of Plasmodium in red cells. A biting mosquito takes gametocytes up, and the cycle goes on in the mosquito.
    Gangrene
    A clinical term for necrosis of a limb or part, usually from loss of blood supply.
    Germinal centre
    The pale centre of a lymphoid follicle, where activated B cells divide fast and switch and mutate their immunoglobulin genes. Burkitt lymphoma arises from a germinal centre B cell.
    Gluconeogenesis
    The making of new glucose, mainly in the liver, from sources such as lactate and amino acids. Insulin suppresses it, and metformin reduces it.
    GLUT4
    The glucose transporter of muscle and fat cells. Insulin moves it from inside the cell to the cell membrane, so these cells take up glucose.
    Glycation
    Binding of glucose to a protein without an enzyme. The higher the blood glucose, the faster it happens.
    Granuloma
    A compact collection of epithelioid macrophages, often with multinucleate giant cells, ringed by lymphocytes.
    Growth fraction
    The share of a tumour's cells that are actively passing through the cell cycle. In Burkitt lymphoma it's nearly all of them.
    HACEK group
    Haemophilus, Actinobacillus, Cardiobacterium, Eikenella and Kingella: commensals of the mouth that are less common causes of infective endocarditis.
    Haemoglobinopathy
    An inherited disorder caused by a mutation that changes the structure of a globin chain, such as sickle haemoglobin (Hb S) or Hb C.
    Haemosiderin
    A golden-brown pigment of stored iron, seen where red cells have broken down or iron is in excess.
    Haemozoin (malarial pigment)
    A brown-black pigment the parasite leaves from the haemoglobin it digests. Ruptured red cells release it, and macrophages in the spleen, liver and marrow take it up.
    Haptoglobin
    A plasma protein that binds free haemoglobin. The pair is removed from the circulation, so the haptoglobin level falls in haemolysis.
    Haemoglobin electrophoresis
    A test that moves haemoglobins along a strip in an electric field, so each kind shows as a band at its own position.
    Heart failure cell
    A macrophage in the alveoli laden with haemosiderin from red cells that leaked out of congested lung capillaries.
    Hepcidin
    A hormone that keeps iron inside macrophages and blocks its absorption from the gut. Inflammation raises it, which starves the marrow of iron.
    Heterophile antibody
    An antibody made in infectious mononucleosis that agglutinates sheep red cells. The Monospot test detects it.
    Holoendemic malaria
    Malaria transmission that runs almost all year, so children are bitten by infected mosquitoes again and again from infancy.
    Howell-Jolly body
    A small remnant of DNA inside a red cell. Howell-Jolly bodies show on the blood film when the spleen has stopped working.
    High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)
    A test that passes haemoglobin through a column. Each kind leaves at its own time, so the machine names it and measures its share of the total.
    HRP2 (histidine-rich protein 2)
    A protein made by P. falciparum and no other species, and the antigen most malaria rapid diagnostic tests detect.
    Hyaline arteriolosclerosis
    Thickening of an arteriole's wall by glassy, pink, structureless material leaked from the plasma, which narrows the lumen. Hypertension, age and diabetes mellitus cause it.
    Hyperplasia
    An increase in the number of cells in a tissue, in cells that can divide.
    Hypersplenism
    An enlarged spleen with a fall in at least one blood cell line while the bone marrow works normally.
    Hypertrophy
    An increase in the size of cells, and so of the organ, without more cells.
    Hypnozoite
    A dormant liver form of P. vivax and P. ovale. Hypnozoites can wake weeks or years later and cause a relapse.
    Hypoglycaemia unawareness
    Loss of the autonomic warning signs of a low glucose after frequent episodes of hypoglycaemia, so the patient doesn't act until confusion or coma sets in.
    Hypoxia
    Too little oxygen reaching the cells.
    Immunohistochemistry
    Staining a tissue section with labelled antibodies, so cells that carry a given protein turn brown. The tissue's architecture is kept.
    Immunophenotype
    The set of proteins a cell carries, shown with labelled antibodies. In a lymphoma it tells you which kind of lymphocyte the tumour copies, and at which stage.
    Impaired fasting glucose (IFG)
    A fasting venous plasma glucose from 6.1 up to 6.9 mmol/L, with a 2-hour OGTT glucose below 7.8. A stage between normal and diabetes mellitus.
    Impaired glucose tolerance (IGT)
    A 2-hour OGTT venous plasma glucose from 7.8 up to 11.1 mmol/L, with a fasting glucose below 7.0. Some people with it go on to develop diabetes mellitus.
    Incretin
    A gut hormone released after a meal that boosts insulin release from the β cells. GLP-1 and GIP are the two main incretins, and DPP-4 breaks them down.
    Infarct
    An area of ischaemic necrosis caused by loss of the blood supply, in sickle cell disease usually from vessels blocked by sickled cells.
    International normalised ratio (INR)
    The prothrombin time corrected for the thromboplastin reagent used, so a result means the same in any laboratory. A normal INR is about 1.
    Insulin resistance
    Failure of target tissues, mainly the liver, skeletal muscle and fat, to respond normally to insulin.
    Insulitis
    Infiltration of the pancreatic islets by lymphocytes, mainly T cells, while they destroy the β cells in type 1 diabetes.
    Intermittent preventive therapy
    Treatment doses of an antimalarial given at set times to people at high risk, such as pregnant women, whether or not they're infected.
    Intrathecal
    Injected into the CSF, so a drug reaches tumour cells in the central nervous system that drugs in the blood barely reach.
    Iron chelation
    Treatment with a drug that binds iron tightly so it can leave the body in urine or faeces. It treats the iron overload of repeated blood transfusion.
    Irreversibly sickled cell
    A red cell whose membrane has been damaged by many cycles of sickling, so it stays sickled even when it's carrying oxygen.
    Ischaemia
    Too little blood reaching a tissue: less oxygen, and less of everything else the blood brings and takes away.
    Karyolysis
    A nucleus fading away as its DNA is broken down.
    Karyorrhexis
    A pyknotic nucleus breaking into fragments.
    Karyotype
    A picture of all a cell's chromosomes, laid out in pairs. It needs dividing cells, so the cells are grown in culture first.
    ATP-sensitive potassium channel
    A potassium channel in the β cell membrane that closes when ATP rises. Closing it depolarises the cell and leads to insulin release. Sulfonylureas close it directly.
    Diabetic ketoacidosis
    Metabolic acidosis from ketone bodies, which the liver makes from fatty acids when there's too little insulin to stop fat breaking down.
    Ki-67
    A nuclear protein present in cells that are in the cell cycle. A Ki-67 stain gives the share of tumour cells dividing, called the proliferation index.
    Kussmaul breathing
    Deep, sighing breathing in metabolic acidosis. It blows off carbon dioxide, which lessens the fall in pH.
    Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)
    An enzyme that turns lactate into pyruvate and back. It's found in many tissues and leaks into the plasma from damaged cells, so a raised level shows cell damage but not where.
    Lactic acidosis
    A high anion gap metabolic acidosis from lactate building up in the blood. Its commonest cause is tissue short of oxygen, from poor perfusion, hypoxia or severe anaemia.
    Lancefield group
    A letter (A, B, D and others) given to a streptococcus by the C carbohydrate antigen in its cell wall.
    Langhans giant cell
    A multinucleate cell formed when macrophages fuse. Its nuclei sit in a horseshoe around the edge of the cell.
    Latency I
    The most restricted pattern of Epstein-Barr virus gene expression, with EBNA1 and the EBER RNAs and little else. Burkitt lymphoma cells show this pattern.
    Latent infection
    An infection in which the viral DNA stays inside the cell and makes few or no new virus particles. Epstein-Barr virus keeps its DNA in the nucleus as a circle.
    Light chain restriction
    Expression of only kappa or only lambda light chain by a population of B cells. Light chain restriction shows the cells are one clone: each B cell makes one light chain, but a normal, polyclonal population carries both, kappa more often than lambda.
    Lipofuscin
    A yellow-brown 'wear and tear' pigment of oxidised lipid, building up in long-lived cells with age.
    Lipohypertrophy
    A swelling of fatty tissue under the skin where insulin is injected into the same site again and again. Insulin is absorbed unevenly from it.
    Liquefactive necrosis
    Necrosis in which enzymes digest the dead tissue into a liquid.
    M protein
    A major virulence factor of group A streptococcus: a wall protein that blocks complement activation and protects the organism from phagocytosis.
    Malignant otitis externa
    Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection of the external ear canal that burrows into the mastoid bone, mostly in older people with diabetes mellitus.
    Merozoite
    The form of Plasmodium released when an infected liver cell or red cell bursts. Each merozoite invades a red cell.
    Metabolic syndrome
    A cluster of central obesity, high triglyceride, low HDL cholesterol, raised blood pressure and raised fasting glucose, linked by insulin resistance.
    Metaplasia
    The replacement of one mature cell type by another, better able to stand a particular stress.
    Metastatic calcification
    Calcium deposited in normal tissue because the blood calcium is high.
    Methaemoglobinaemia
    Haemoglobin in the blood with its iron oxidised from the Fe2+ to the Fe3+ state. Drugs such as primaquine can cause it, and the patient looks cyanosed.
    Microalbuminuria
    A urine albumin of 30 to 300 mg a day, too little for overt proteinuria. In diabetes mellitus it's one of the earliest signs of nephropathy.
    Diabetic microangiopathy
    Disease of small blood vessels in diabetes mellitus: capillary basement membranes become thick, yet the capillaries leak plasma proteins.
    Molecular mimicry
    An immune response to a microbe that also attacks the body's own tissue, because a microbial antigen looks like a self antigen.
    Mucormycosis (zygomycosis)
    Infection with moulds such as Rhizopus and Mucor, whose hyphae have no septa, branch at right angles and invade blood vessel walls.
    Multidrug-resistant (MDR)
    Resistant to antibiotics from several different classes at once, such as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus.
    Mural thrombus
    A thrombus attached to the wall of a heart chamber or of the aorta.
    Mycotic aneurysm
    An aneurysm caused by bacterial infection of an artery wall, where an infected embolus has lodged.
    Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase (the sodium pump)
    The membrane pump that moves sodium out of a cell and potassium in, using ATP. Digoxin inhibits it in heart muscle, and potassium competes with digoxin for it.
    Necrosis
    Death of cells in a living tissue, with their membranes breaking and their contents leaking out, which brings in inflammation.
    Necrotising granuloma
    A granuloma whose centre has died. In tuberculosis the dead centre is caseous.
    Nephrosclerosis
    Diffuse scarring of the kidney from narrowed arteries and arterioles, which leaves a finely granular surface and a thin cortex.
    Neurogenic bladder
    A bladder that doesn't empty properly because the nerves that control it are damaged. In diabetes mellitus autonomic neuropathy causes it, and urine left standing lets bacteria multiply.
    Neuroglycopenia
    Brain dysfunction from too little glucose: faintness, dizziness and confusion, which can progress to coma.
    Nodular glomerulosclerosis (Kimmelstiel-Wilson lesion)
    Ball-like nodules of laminated matrix at the edge of the glomerulus, almost pathognomonic of diabetes mellitus.
    Non-Hodgkin lymphoma
    Any malignant tumour of lymphocytes other than Hodgkin lymphoma. Most arise from B cells, and Burkitt lymphoma is one of them.
    Oncogene
    A gene that drives cell growth and division when it's mutated or overexpressed. MYC is the oncogene switched on in Burkitt lymphoma.
    Opsonisation
    Coating of a microbe with antibody or complement so phagocytes can recognise and ingest it.
    Osmotic diuresis
    A large flow of urine caused by a solute, such as glucose, that stays in the renal tubule and holds water with it.
    Osteomyelitis
    Infection of bone and its marrow. In sickle cell disease bacteria seed bone already damaged by infarction.
    P-selectin
    An adhesion molecule that helps sickle cells stick to the walls of blood vessels, one step in vaso-occlusion.
    Pancarditis
    Inflammation of all three layers of the heart: the pericardium, the myocardium and the endocardium, including the valves.
    Pancytopenia
    A fall in all three blood cell lines: red cells, white cells and platelets.
    Papillary necrosis (necrotising papillitis)
    Death of the renal papillae, the tips of the medullary pyramids, as a form of acute pyelonephritis. Diabetes mellitus, urinary obstruction and sickle cell anaemia are its main settings.
    Paraprotein
    One immunoglobulin made in excess by a single clone of B cells. Serum protein electrophoresis shows it as a narrow, dense band, usually in the gamma region.
    Parasitaemia
    Malaria parasites in the circulating blood, counted on a film as the share of red cells infected or the number per microlitre.
    Penicillin-binding protein (PBP)
    A bacterial enzyme that cross-links the peptide chains of peptidoglycan, which gives the cell wall its strength. Beta-lactam antibiotics bind its active site and block it.
    Pigment gallstone
    A gallstone made of calcium bilirubinate. It forms when bile carries a high load of unconjugated bilirubin, as in chronic haemolysis.
    pLDH (Plasmodium lactate dehydrogenase)
    An enzyme of the malaria parasite, detected by some rapid diagnostic tests. pLDH tests turn negative within days of cure.
    Polyclonal
    Coming from many different B-cell clones at once, rather than from one. Malaria and early Epstein-Barr virus infection both drive polyclonal B-cell activation.
    Polyclonal response
    Immunoglobulins made by many B-cell clones at once, as in chronic infection. Serum protein electrophoresis shows it as a broad, diffuse rise in the gamma region.
    Polymerisation of Hb S
    Deoxygenated sickle haemoglobin molecules binding one another into long fibres. The fibres distort the red cell into a sickle shape.
    Polymicrobial infection
    Infection with more than one organism at the same time. Infection that spreads into tissue from an open wound is often polymicrobial.
    Polyol (sorbitol) pathway
    Conversion of glucose to sorbitol by aldose reductase. It uses up NADPH, so glutathione can't be regenerated and the cell is left open to oxidative stress.
    PPAR-γ
    Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma, a nuclear receptor in muscle, fat and liver that controls genes for glucose and fat handling. Pioglitazone activates it.
    Prodrug
    A drug given in an inactive form that the body converts into the active drug. Cyclophosphamide is one: liver cytochrome P450 enzymes activate it.
    Prothrombin time (PT)
    The time citrated plasma takes to clot after thromboplastin and calcium are added. It tests the extrinsic and common clotting pathways.
    Pseudohyperkalaemia
    A potassium result raised in the tube, not in the patient: potassium leaks out of cells after the blood is taken, as in a haemolysed sample or one left unseparated.
    Pseudohypoglycaemia
    A falsely low glucose result, because blood cells kept using glucose in a tube with no fluoride before the plasma was separated.
    Purine
    A nitrogen-containing base of DNA and RNA: adenine or guanine. In humans, purines that aren't reused are broken down to urate.
    Pyknosis
    A nucleus that has shrunk and stains dark, as its chromatin condenses.
    Pyuria
    Neutrophils in the urine, which can make it look turbid. It's a sign of urinary infection such as acute pyelonephritis.
    Radical cure
    Treatment that clears malaria parasites from both the blood and the liver, so P. vivax or P. ovale can't relapse. It needs a blood-stage drug plus primaquine or tafenoquine.
    Recrudescence
    The return of malaria from blood-stage parasites that treatment didn't clear. A relapse is different: it comes from dormant liver forms.
    Renal threshold for glucose
    The plasma glucose, about 10 mmol/L, above which the renal tubules can't reabsorb all the filtered glucose, so glucose appears in the urine.
    Ischaemia–reperfusion injury
    Further injury to ischaemic tissue when its blood supply comes back.
    Reticulocyte
    A young red cell, just out of the marrow, that still holds RNA. The reticulocyte count shows how well the marrow is making red cells.
    Reye syndrome
    An illness of children and adolescents linked to taking aspirin during a viral infection, with or without fever. Aspirin isn't given to children or adolescents with a viral illness.
    Ribonucleotide reductase
    The enzyme that turns ribonucleotides into the deoxyribonucleotides a cell needs to copy its DNA. Blocking it stops dividing cells in the S phase of the cell cycle.
    Ring abscess
    An abscess in the tissue around a valve ring, where an infective vegetation has eroded into the myocardium beneath it.
    Ring haemorrhage
    A small haemorrhage in the brain's white matter arranged as a ring around a small vessel, seen at autopsy in cerebral malaria.
    Rouleaux
    Red cells stacked like a pile of coins. Fibrinogen and immunoglobulins cause the stacking, and the stacks settle fast, raising the ESR.
    Sanctuary site
    A tissue that drugs in the blood reach poorly, such as the brain and the testes, so tumour cells there can survive treatment.
    Schizont
    A Plasmodium form whose nucleus has divided many times. It breaks up into merozoites when the liver cell or red cell bursts.
    Schüffner's dots
    Fine dots in a red cell infected with P. vivax or P. ovale, seen on a well-stained Giemsa film as the parasite matures.
    Senescence
    A permanent stop to cell division that cells reach with age.
    Sequestration
    Red cells carrying maturing P. falciparum sticking to the endothelium of small vessels, through the parasite protein PfEMP1, so they leave the circulating blood and block the capillaries.
    SGLT2 (sodium-glucose co-transporter 2)
    The transporter in the proximal tubule that reabsorbs most of the glucose filtered by the kidney. SGLT2 inhibitors block it, so glucose is lost in the urine.
    Siderofibrotic nodule
    A small nodule of fibrous tissue holding iron pigment and calcium, found in the scarred spleen of sickle cell disease beside vessels full of sickled cells.
    Hepatic sinusoidal lymphocytosis
    Lymphocytes filling the sinusoids of the liver on biopsy. It supports a diagnosis of tropical splenomegaly syndrome but isn't specific to it.
    Solubility product
    The highest product of two ion concentrations that a solution can hold. Above it, the salt comes out of solution, as calcium phosphate does when plasma phosphate is high.
    Somatic hypermutation
    Random mutation of the variable-region genes of immunoglobulin in dividing germinal centre B cells. Cells whose new antibody binds antigen better are selected to survive.
    Pitting
    The spleen's removal of inclusions, such as Howell-Jolly bodies and Heinz bodies, from red cells it then lets go back into the blood.
    Splenic sequestration
    Sudden pooling of sickled red cells in the spleen, which enlarges while the haemoglobin falls. Seen mostly in infants with sickle cell disease.
    Splenomegaly
    Enlargement of the spleen. A massive spleen can reach the right iliac fossa.
    Sporozoite
    The form of Plasmodium that an infected female Anopheles mosquito injects when it bites. Sporozoites travel in the blood to the liver.
    Starry-sky pattern
    Pale tingible body macrophages scattered through sheets of dark tumour cells, seen at low power. Typical of Burkitt lymphoma, but not specific to it.
    Steatosis (fatty change)
    Triglyceride building up inside cells, seen most in the liver.
    Streptolysin O
    An oxygen-labile toxin of group A streptococcus that lyses red and white cells. It's antigenic, so infection is followed by anti-streptolysin O antibodies.
    Sydenham chorea
    A neurological disorder of acute rheumatic fever with involuntary, purposeless, rapid movements.
    Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT)
    A DNA polymerase made by precursor B and T cells, before they mature. A positive TdT stain marks a lymphoblast.
    Tingible body macrophage
    A benign macrophage that has eaten the nuclear debris of apoptotic cells. In Burkitt lymphoma these macrophages form the pale stars of the starry sky.
    Tissue schizonticide
    An antimalarial drug that kills the parasites developing or lying dormant in the liver, such as primaquine.
    Chromosomal translocation
    A break in two chromosomes with an exchange of the broken pieces. In Burkitt lymphoma, t(8;14) moves MYC on chromosome 8 next to the immunoglobulin heavy-chain gene on chromosome 14.
    Trophozoite
    The growing stage of Plasmodium inside a red cell. On a stained film the young trophozoite looks like a ring.
    Tumour lysis syndrome
    The chemical upset when many tumour cells die at once, usually as treatment starts: urate, potassium and phosphate rise, calcium falls, and the kidneys can fail.
    Tumour marker
    A substance in the blood that a tumour makes or releases. In some tumours its level relates to the tumour's mass or spread, and a fall or a new rise can follow treatment and recurrence.
    Type II hypersensitivity
    Tissue injury caused by IgG or IgM antibodies that bind antigens fixed on cells or in the tissue matrix.
    Type III hypersensitivity
    Tissue injury caused by antigen-antibody (immune) complexes that deposit in vessel walls or glomeruli and activate complement.
    Unconjugated bilirubin
    Bilirubin that the liver hasn't yet joined to glucuronate. It isn't water-soluble, so it travels in the plasma bound to albumin and doesn't pass into the urine.
    Urobilinogen
    A colourless product that gut bacteria make from bilirubin. Some is absorbed and sent back into bile by the liver, and a little passes in the urine.
    Vaso-occlusion
    Blockage of small blood vessels by sickled red cells, which causes ischaemia, pain and infarction in the tissue they supply.
    Vegetation
    A thrombus on a heart valve. It may be sterile, or hold organisms, as in infective endocarditis.
    Verrucae (rheumatic vegetations)
    Small, warty vegetations in a row along the lines of closure of a valve in acute rheumatic fever, made of fibrinoid necrosis and fibrin.
    Villous lymphocyte
    A lymphocyte with fine hair-like projections from its cytoplasm. Villous lymphocytes in the blood suggest splenic marginal zone lymphoma.
    Virchow's triad
    The three causes of thrombosis: endothelial injury, stasis or turbulent blood flow, and hypercoagulability of the blood.
    Viridans streptococci
    Alpha-haemolytic streptococci that are normal flora of the mouth. They're the usual cause of subacute infective endocarditis on a damaged valve.
    Vitamin K epoxide reductase (VKOR)
    The liver enzyme that turns used vitamin K epoxide back into active vitamin K, so clotting factors can go on being carboxylated. Warfarin blocks it. Its gene is VKORC1.
    Whipple's triad
    A low plasma glucose, neuroglycopenic symptoms, and relief of the symptoms when the glucose is raised. Together they show that symptoms come from hypoglycaemia.
    Xanthine oxidase
    The liver enzyme that oxidises hypoxanthine to xanthine, and xanthine to urate. Allopurinol inhibits it.
